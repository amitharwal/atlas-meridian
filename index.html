      About this data

      Every September, each UN member gives a speech at the General Assembly's General Debate. Atlas Meridian reads every one of them, from 1946 to 2025 (over 11,000 speeches), and maps what each country chose to talk about.

      How it works

      Each speech is split into short passages of about 60 words. A small language model sorts each passage into one of 16 topics, or into none if it's ceremonial (thanking the President, and so on). A country's share for a topic is the percentage of its passages sorted into that topic.

      "Speaks most like" compares the overall wording of speeches in the same year. It measures talking about the same things, not friendship: in 1985 the USA's closest match was the Soviet Union, because both spoke mostly about each other and about arms control.

      What the numbers show

      Eight World Bank measures sit alongside the speeches, each paired with a speech topic, so you can compare what a country says with what the numbers show: GDP per person (development), CO₂ emissions per person (climate), life expectancy (health), women in parliament (gender), internet users (technology), foreign debt (debt), trade (trade) and undernourishment (food). They are updated automatically each month.

      Official figures often arrive a year or two late. Where the chosen year has no figure yet, the map shows the most recent one from up to two years earlier and says so. Foreign debt is only reported for low- and middle-income countries, and some places, such as Taiwan, aren't covered by the World Bank.

      How accurate is it?

      When checked against hand-sorted passages, the model agrees about 71% of the time. It's strongest on peace, democracy, development and gender, and weakest on terrorism, debt and food. Treat single numbers as rough and trends as more reliable. The hand-sorted passages used to train and test it were labelled by an AI assistant (Claude), not by human experts.

      Things to know

      • Speeches before 1992 by the Soviet Union appear on Russia, which took over its UN seat. Countries that no longer exist without a successor seat (Yugoslavia, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, South Yemen) have no shape on the map, but you'll still find them in "Speaks most like".
      • Speeches given in other languages are official UN English translations. The 2024 speeches were translated by an AI model (GPT-4o), and the 2025 speeches were transcribed from video by an AI model (Whisper), so they may contain small errors.
      • Borders are for display only and don't imply any position on disputed territory.
