    About Atlas Meridian

    Atlas Meridian is a free world map of country statistics. Pick any of {{indicators}} measures (population, the economy, health, education, the environment, technology and more), slide through the years since 1960, and click a country for its key facts, its trend and how it compares with the rest of the world. The Rankings page lists every country for any measure, and Compare puts two countries side by side.

    Where the numbers come from

    All the statistics come from World Bank Open Data, which collects them from the UN, the IMF, the WHO, UNESCO and other agencies (each measure names its original source). They are updated automatically each month.

    Official figures often arrive a year or two late. Where the chosen year has no figure yet, the map shows the most recent one from up to two years earlier and says so. Surveys run every few years, such as inequality, poverty and literacy, may show a figure up to five years old. Some places, such as Taiwan, aren't covered by the World Bank. "World median" is the middle country that year, not a population-weighted average.

    What countries say at the UN

    Every September, each UN member gives a speech at the General Assembly's General Debate. The "At the UN" maps read every one of them, from {{firstYear}} to {{lastYear}} (over {{speeches}} speeches), and show what each country chose to talk about.

    Each speech is split into short passages of about 60 words. A small language model sorts each passage into one of {{topics}} topics, or into none if it's ceremonial (thanking the President, and so on). A country's share for a topic is the percentage of its passages sorted into that topic.

    "Speaks most like" compares the overall wording of speeches in the same year. It measures talking about the same things, not friendship: in 1985 the USA's closest match was the Soviet Union, because both spoke mostly about each other and about arms control.

    Who spoke, and who talks about whom

    Speakers' names come from the corpus. Their titles are recorded consistently from 1994, and are grouped into four ranks (head of state or government, deputy leader, foreign minister, other).

    "Who mentions…" counts how many times each speech names a country, including UN formal names and former names (Zaire, Kampuchea). It counts names, not adjectives ("Israel", not "Israeli"), and skips names that could mean two countries, such as "Congo" or "Korea" on their own.

    How accurate are the speech topics?

    When checked against hand-sorted passages, the model agrees about 71% of the time. It's strongest on peace, democracy, development and gender, and weakest on terrorism, debt and food. Treat single numbers as rough and trends as more reliable. The hand-sorted passages used to train and test it were labelled by an AI assistant, not by human experts.

    Things to know

    • Speeches before 1992 by the Soviet Union appear on Russia, which took over its UN seat. Countries that no longer exist without a successor seat (Yugoslavia, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, South Yemen) have no shape on the map, but you'll still find them in "Speaks most like".
    • Speeches given in other languages are official UN English translations. The 2024 speeches were translated by an AI model (GPT-4o), and the 2025 speeches were transcribed from video by an AI model (Whisper), so they may contain small errors.
    • Borders are for display only and don't imply any position on disputed territory.

    Sources
